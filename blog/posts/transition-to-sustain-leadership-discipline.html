Make transition-to-sustain a leadership discipline

The readiness model I used across 45+ application transitions to replace late hand-offs with explicit ownership, evidence, hypercare, and acceptance.

By Sachin Kapoor30 August 20266 min read
Experience context
PepsiCo GCC
Transformation scope
45+ application transitions

Many production problems are designed months before go-live. They begin when operational readiness is treated as the final phase of delivery instead of a product responsibility from the start.

At PepsiCo GCC, I standardized Transition to Sustain and Release to Sustain across 45+ applications. The work involved product, engineering, security, infrastructure, SRE, service management, application support, business stakeholders, and partners. My leadership role was to turn those contributions into one evidence-based acceptance model.

Start with the outcome, not the checklist

The outcome of transition is not a completed knowledge-transfer calendar. It is a support organization that can operate the service safely, diagnose problems, execute routine changes, communicate with users, and improve the service without depending on the project team for every decision.

That definition changes the conversation. A document can exist and still be unusable. A monitoring tool can be installed and still miss the signal that matters. A support roster can be staffed and still lack decision rights. Readiness must therefore be demonstrated, not declared.

Use readiness dimensions that expose risk

I organized readiness around a small number of durable dimensions. The exact artifact may vary by application, but the questions should remain consistent.

  • Service and business contextWho uses the service, what is critical, when are the peak periods, and what business consequence follows an outage?
  • Architecture and dependenciesCan the support team explain the service boundary, upstream and downstream dependencies, data flows, failure modes, and recovery path?
  • Observability and reliabilityAre health checks, dashboards, alerts, logs, SLOs, escalation paths, backup, and recovery controls proven in realistic scenarios?
  • Service managementAre incident, request, problem, change, release, knowledge, SLA, and communication procedures connected to real owners?
  • Security and release controlAre access, vulnerability, quality, approval, deployment, rollback, and evidence requirements built into the delivery path?
  • People, partners, and commercialsAre staffing, on-call expectations, RACI, partner deliverables, licenses, renewals, and support obligations clear?

Move from artifact collection to proof

A transition team can produce a large document repository and still leave the sustain team unprepared. I prefer scenario-based proof. Ask the receiving team to trace a failed transaction. Let them execute a release and rollback. Run an incident simulation. Confirm that an alert reaches the correct responder. Test the executive and user communication path.

These exercises reveal the quality of knowledge transfer and the gaps between teams. They also build confidence. The sustain team becomes an active participant in readiness rather than the recipient of a late hand-off.

Create explicit gates and exceptions

I use checkpoints to make readiness visible: early discovery, operational design, evidence review, acceptance, release readiness, and hypercare exit. Each checkpoint has an accountable owner and a clear decision - accept, accept with owned risk, or do not proceed.

Not every risk must block release. But every material exception should have a named owner, treatment plan, due date, and consequence that leaders understand. This protects the team from informal pressure and protects the business from invisible risk.

Make hypercare a learning period

Hypercare should not be an indefinite extension of the project team. I define entry and exit conditions, the support model during the period, the defects or knowledge gaps being closed, and the evidence needed for final acceptance. Incidents, user questions, deployment observations, and monitoring gaps become inputs to the runbook and backlog.

The same discipline applies to Release to Sustain. Operational readiness is not completed once for an application and forgotten. Every material release can change the dependency map, alerting needs, capacity profile, recovery procedure, or support knowledge.

A transition is complete when the receiving team can operate the service with confidence - not when the project team has finished presenting it.

Closing perspective

Standardization does not mean giving every application the same documents. It means giving leaders and teams the same language for risk, evidence, ownership, and acceptance.

Across 45+ transitions, the most important shift was cultural: sustain teams engaged earlier, engineering teams designed for operability, and leaders made conscious decisions with visible evidence. That is why I treat Transition to Sustain as a leadership discipline, not a hand-off checklist.
