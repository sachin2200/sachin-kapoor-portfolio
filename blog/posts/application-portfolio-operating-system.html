Leadership

From application ownership to an operating system

How I connect governance, engineering, reliability, financials, and people decisions while leading a 25+ application portfolio.

By Sachin Kapoor30 August 20267 min read
Experience context
PepsiCo GCC
Leadership scope
25+ applications · 25+ FTEs · 35+ partner resources

Application portfolios do not become manageable simply because every application has an owner. At enterprise scale, the real challenge is connecting decisions that are usually made in separate rooms: delivery priorities, production health, technical risk, financial commitments, partner performance, and team capacity.

At PepsiCo GCC, my responsibility spans a 25+ application portfolio within a wider landscape of 40+ mission-critical platforms. I lead multidisciplinary teams across engineering, SRE, DevSecOps, AI, and application operations. The most useful lesson from that scope is straightforward: portfolio leadership needs an operating system, not a larger status report.

Build one frame for decisions

I use a connected leadership frame with five lenses. Each lens matters independently, but the value appears when we examine the trade-offs between them.

  • Business value and commitmentsWhat outcome is the application enabling, which commitments are time-bound, and what happens if we delay?
  • Delivery and engineering flowAre teams making predictable progress, and are dependencies, quality, and release readiness visible early?
  • Reliability and service healthWhat do incidents, SLOs, observability, problem records, and operational readiness tell us about customer risk?
  • Financial and commercial controlAre cloud consumption, licenses, partner capacity, SOW milestones, and renewals aligned to actual value?
  • People and capabilityDo we have clear ownership, healthy capacity, succession depth, and a plan to close the skills that tomorrow's portfolio will require?

This frame prevents a familiar failure mode: a project can look green on schedule while production risk, technical debt, or partner dependency is quietly increasing. A common language lets leaders see the whole system before those signals become an escalation.

Turn reviews into a control loop

I present Operations and Engineering Reviews using data from Power BI, Azure DevOps, and ServiceNow. The dashboard is not the review. It is the shared evidence that allows the review to focus on decisions.

A productive review asks: What changed since the last cycle? Which signal needs intervention? Who owns the next action? What evidence will show that the risk is reducing? Those questions create a loop between observation, decision, action, and learning.

I separate the cadence by decision horizon. Teams need frequent operational conversations. Application owners need a cross-functional view of reliability and change. Portfolio leaders need trends, material risks, capacity choices, and financial consequences. Mixing all three into one meeting produces detail without control.

Make ownership layered and explicit

Ownership is not a name in a spreadsheet. It is a set of decision rights. I distinguish application ownership, service ownership, engineering ownership, and portfolio accountability. The same person may cover more than one layer, but the responsibilities should still be explicit.

This becomes especially important across internal teams and partner resources. Clear RACI, service boundaries, acceptance criteria, escalation paths, and SOW deliverables reduce ambiguity without creating bureaucracy. The aim is not to centralize every decision with the portfolio leader. It is to make the right decision easy at the lowest responsible level.

Use metrics as prompts, not verdicts

DORA indicators, service metrics, financial data, delivery flow, and operational trends all matter. None of them should be used in isolation to label a team. I use metrics to open a conversation: what condition produced this trend, what constraint is the team experiencing, and which system change will improve the outcome?

This approach creates accountability without encouraging teams to optimize a number at the expense of the service. It also changes leadership behavior. Instead of asking for more reporting, leaders can remove dependencies, clarify priorities, secure sponsorship, or accept a conscious risk.

A portfolio becomes governable when every review connects evidence to a decision, every decision has an owner, and every owner knows what outcome will prove progress.

Closing perspective

The scale of a portfolio is not the number of applications. It is the number of relationships between business outcomes, technology, services, partners, finances, and people. My role is to make those relationships visible and create a leadership rhythm that turns visibility into action.

That is the operating system I continue to refine: clear decision frames, layered ownership, evidence-based reviews, and teams trusted to act. It is how a collection of applications begins to operate as a strategic capability.
